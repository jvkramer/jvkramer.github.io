Who enters, and what they earn

Proposition 2 says that the sons who enter an occupation are selected on talent, and by how much. Proposition 3 turns that into their earnings. Both rest on one property of the Fréchet distribution, which the last section takes apart.

The setup

A son of type \((g,x)\), with \(g\) his father's occupation and \(x\) his enlistment scores, takes the occupation \(i\) with the largest \(\tilde w_{ig}(x)\,\epsilon_i\,\mu_i\). The reward index \(\tilde w_{ig}\) collects pay, productivity, preferences and the family wedges. In his father's occupation it is higher by \(d+t\) in logs: the preference discount plus the pay premium.

A share \(1-\delta\) of sons sort on talent: they draw \(\epsilon_i\) from a Fréchet with shape \(\theta\) (larger \(\theta\), less dispersion) and have no taste draws. The other \(\delta\) sort on taste: they draw \(\mu_i\) from the same Fréchet and are equally talented everywhere. Proposition 1 gives the logit \(p_{ig}=\tilde w_{ig}^{\theta}/m_g\). Throughout, \(\rho=(1-\delta)/\theta\).

Proposition 2, in words

Among the sons who entered \(i\), average log talent is higher the less likely they were to enter, by \(\rho\) per log point of entry probability.

Proposition 3, in words

Their mean log earnings are what a random son of their type would earn in \(i\), plus that selection bonus.

Proposition 2 · HHJK eq. 5

The less likely the entry, the better the draw

Statement
\[ \mathbb{E}\big[\log\epsilon_i \,\big|\, i\text{ chosen},g,x\big]=\text{const}-\rho\,\log p_{ig}(x),\qquad \rho=\frac{1-\delta}{\theta}. \]

Take the note's example: two sons with the same skills, one the son of an office clerk and one the son of a physician. Neither father is related to electrician. The physician's son is pulled harder toward his father's occupation, so he is less likely to become an electrician. Figure 1 shows what that does to the electricians each family produces.

  1. The red density is the grey density moved to the right. Its shape and spread are unchanged. Only its location moves, by exactly \(\tfrac1\theta\log(1/p)\). This is Proposition 2 for talent sorters, and it is a special property of Fréchet talent.
  2. The physician's son is half as likely to become an electrician (0.05 against 0.10). The electricians among physicians' sons are therefore better by \(\tfrac1\theta\log 2\): log points at the current θ.
  3. Move θ. A lower θ spreads the cloud, and the same entry probabilities now imply bigger talent gaps. θ does not change who enters here, because the probabilities are held fixed. It changes how different the entrants are.

Proposition 3 · HHJK eq. 6

Entrants earn their potential plus a selection bonus

Statement, first line
\[ \mathbb{E}\big[\log\text{wage}\,\big|\,i,g,x\big]=\underbrace{\log w_i+\log\bar h_{ig}(x)-\log\tau_{ig}}_{\text{potential earnings}}\;\underbrace{-\,\rho\,\log p_{ig}(x)}_{\text{selection bonus}}\;+\;\text{const}. \]

A son's log wage in \(i\) is log pay per efficiency unit, plus log productivity, minus the log wedge, plus log talent. Average over the sons who entered and use Proposition 2 for the talent term. The first bracket is what a randomly chosen son of type \((g,x)\) would earn in \(i\). The second is Proposition 2's bonus.

Sons of the clerk and the physician have the same potential earnings as electricians (Assumptions 1–3: same residual talent, same productivity, no family wedge in an unrelated occupation). So their electricians differ only through the bonus, and they lie on one line. The electrician's own son is different twice over: he enters more easily, and he may be paid a premium \(t\).

  1. The raw gap compares a less selected son (the follower) with a more selected one (the clerk's son). It understates \(t\), and at the defaults it even has the wrong sign. Adding back \(\rho\log(p_{\text{follower}}/p_{\text{clerk}})\) recovers \(t\) exactly. That is the formula for \(t\) in Proposition 4 of the note.
  2. Press δ = 1. The line goes flat: taste sorters are not selected on talent, so there is no bonus, and the raw gap is \(t\). This is the submitted paper's world, and the editor's selection question is whether the line has a slope.
  3. Press δ = 0, then move t. The raw gap barely moves. A higher premium draws in more followers, each less selected, and the two effects cancel up to a small option-value term. What is left in the raw gap is about \(-d\): followers took the job with worse draws because they liked it more. Earnings alone would not reveal \(t\) here; the correction does.
Statement, second line
\[ \mathbb{E}\big[\log\text{wage}\,\big|\,i,g,x\big]=\text{const}+\delta\log\frac{w_i\,\bar h_{ig}(x)}{\tau_{ig}}-(1-\delta)\log z_{ig}(x)+\rho\log m_g(x). \]

The second line is the same equation after substituting Proposition 1 for \(\log p\). It says how entrants' earnings respond when something in \(i\) changes. Anything that raises pay, productivity or the wedge passes through with weight \(\delta\) only. The rest is eaten by weaker selection. Figure 4 shows it for a rise in electrician pay.

The distributional assumption

What Fréchet buys

Talent is Fréchet: \(\Pr(\epsilon_i\le e)=\exp(-e^{-\theta})\), drawn independently across occupations. Equivalently, \(\log\epsilon_i\) is Gumbel with spread \(1/\theta\). In one sentence, Fréchet lets choices and earnings be linked by a single number. A son's own entry probability is all you need to know about how selected he is. The selection is linear in its log, and the slope is \(1/\theta\), the inverse of the same \(\theta\) that turns log-odds into log earnings.

The key fact behind Propositions 2 and 3 is in the appendix proof of Proposition 2. The best value a son can get, \(Y=\max_s\tilde w_{sg}\epsilon_s\), has the same distribution whichever occupation delivers it. An entrant's talent is \(\epsilon_i=Y/\tilde w_{ig}\), so it depends on occupation \(i\) only through \(\tilde w_{ig}\), that is, through \(p_{ig}\). Figure 5 checks this by simulation.

What it costs

  • Independence of irrelevant alternatives. In the counterfactual that removes physicians' following discount, some physicians' sons who would have become physicians choose something else. The model splits them across all other occupations in proportion to how often physicians' sons with the same skills already choose each one: every other occupation's share is scaled up by the same factor. A son who leaves medicine is as likely, relative to current shares, to become an electrician as a dentist. In reality those who leave medicine probably have tastes and talents suited to nearby occupations, so more would go there. Conditioning on skills \(x\) and the tier-1 and tier-2 discounts soften this; correlated draws within one-digit groups (nested Fréchet) would fix it.
  • One θ for all occupations. Selection is equally strong in every occupation. The note's test is \(\rho\) by one-digit group.
  • Independent draws across occupations. A son good at one manual trade is not more likely to be good at another. Correlated draws (nested Fréchet) lose the closed forms at 91 occupations.
  • The linear selection term is a functional form. The step from unrelated sons (p around 0.05–0.10) to followers (p several times higher) leans on it, as Figure 6 shows. Dahl's flexible \(\lambda(p)\) in sd_selection.m is the robustness check.
  • A long right tail. Gumbel log-talent makes rare entrants very selected, and mean talent exists only for \(\theta>1\).

Source: hhjk_note.tex, Propositions 1–4 and the appendix proofs; Hsieh, Hurst, Jones and Klenow (2019), eqs. 4–6.
